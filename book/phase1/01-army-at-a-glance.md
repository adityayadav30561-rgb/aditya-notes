# The Indian Army at a Glance
~ Role, command, headquarters and the basic vocabulary of the Army

## Role of the Indian Army

The **primary role** of the Indian Army is to defend the country against external aggression: to be ready at all times to fight and win wars, and so protect India's sovereignty and territorial integrity. Everything else the Army does, including its training, its equipment and its peacetime deployments, exists to make it ready for this one task. The Army also works as a deterrent. A strong, ready Army makes an enemy think twice before attacking.

> **Secondary roles.** (1) Help the government deal with proxy war and other internal threats, for example counter-insurgency and counter-terrorism in J&K and the North-East. (2) Provide aid to the civil authority when the state asks for it: maintaining law and order as a last resort, and relief in floods, earthquakes and cyclones. (3) Take part in United Nations peacekeeping missions abroad.

An easy way to remember it: *fight the war first, help the nation second.* If an interviewer asks about the Army's role, begin with war-fighting and only then mention the secondary roles.

## Supreme Commander of the Armed Forces

The **President of India** is the Supreme Commander of the Indian Armed Forces (Army, Navy and Air Force). At present this is Smt Droupadi Murmu (verify). The authority comes from the Constitution: **Article 53(2)** says that the supreme command of the Defence Forces of the Union is vested in the President, and that its exercise is regulated by law.

In practice the President does not take day-to-day military decisions. As with other executive powers, the President acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers. The real direction comes from the elected government through the **Raksha Mantri (Defence Minister)**, the Ministry of Defence and the Cabinet Committee on Security. The President's role is mainly formal, but important: the President appoints the Service Chiefs and the Chief of Defence Staff, and presents the Colours to units.

## Chief of the Army Staff: Head and Rank

The professional head of the Army is the **Chief of the Army Staff (COAS)**, currently Gen Dhiraj Seth (verify; this changes with each new Chief). He advises the government on military matters and commands the Army through Army Headquarters. The Chief serves for three years or until age 62, whichever comes first.

The COAS is a **General**, a four-star rank. The path is unusual and worth knowing. An Army Commander, and the Vice Chief of the Army Staff, are **Lieutenant Generals** (three-star). When a Lt Gen is chosen as COAS, he is **promoted to General**: *one more star is added* to his rank insignia, and he becomes a four-star officer. On retirement he keeps the rank of General.

Above General there is only **Field Marshal**, a five-star rank that is ceremonial and conferred only for exceptional service. India has had two: Field Marshal K.M. Cariappa (1986) and Field Marshal S.H.F.J. Manekshaw (1973).

> **Do not confuse** the COAS with the **Chief of Defence Staff (CDS)**. The CDS is also a four-star General, but heads the Department of Military Affairs and coordinates all three Services. The COAS heads the Army alone.

## Army Headquarters: Location and Functions

Army Headquarters (Integrated HQ of the Ministry of Defence, Army) is in **New Delhi**: South Block and the Delhi Cantonment, where a new complex, Thal Sena Bhawan, has been built (verify). It is the brain of the Army. It works out what the Army must do and tells the Commands how to do it. Its work divides into two kinds.

### Operational functions

These concern fighting wars: planning, strategy, deployment and policy. The **Directorate General of Military Operations (DGMO)** plans operations and is the Army's voice with the government in a crisis. The DGMO also speaks to his Pakistani counterpart on a hotline; in February 2021 the two DGMOs agreed to observe the 2003 ceasefire along the LoC. **Military Intelligence** studies the enemy's strength and intentions. **Perspective Planning** decides what forces India will need ten or fifteen years from now.

### Administrative functions

These concern keeping the Army alive, fed, paid, equipped and trained.
- **Adjutant General's Branch:** recruitment policy, discipline, welfare and soldiers' terms of service. The Agnipath scheme of 2022 came from here.
- **Military Secretary's Branch:** postings and promotions of officers, for example which officer commands a battalion.
- **Master General of Ordnance:** weapons, equipment and ammunition, for example bringing the K9 Vajra and Pinaka rocket systems into service.
- **Quartermaster General:** rations, fuel, clothing and transport, for example stocking Ladakh and Siachen before the winter snow closes the roads.
- **Engineer-in-Chief:** military engineering, such as roads, bridges and barracks.
- **Signal Officer-in-Chief:** communications and information systems.

## Arms and Services

The Army has two kinds of branches, and the difference is simple: **arms fight, services sustain**.

### The arms

The **combat arms** close in with the enemy and fight him face to face: the **Infantry**, the **Armoured Corps** (tanks) and the **Mechanised Infantry** (infantry who travel in armoured vehicles). The **combat support arms** help the combat arms to fight: **Artillery** (fire support), **Engineers** (clear obstacles, build and break), **Signals** (communication), **Army Air Defence** (protect against aircraft and missiles), **Army Aviation** (helicopters) and the **Intelligence Corps**.

### The services

The **services** keep the Army supplied and healthy so that it can fight. They are the **Army Service Corps** (food, fuel and transport), the **Army Ordnance Corps** (stores and ammunition), the **Corps of EME** (repair of vehicles and weapons), the **Army Medical Corps**, the **Army Dental Corps**, the **Remount and Veterinary Corps** and the **Corps of Military Police**.

A tank battle shows how they all fit. The tanks (combat arm) advance; artillery (support) shells the enemy's guns; engineers (support) clear a minefield; signals (support) keep everyone in radio contact; and the ASC (service) brings fuel and shells, and the EME repairs any tank that breaks down.

## Formation, Unit and Sub-unit

These three words appear in every discussion of the Army, and the difference is easy once you see the logic.

A **unit** is the basic, self-contained building block, such as an infantry **battalion** or an armoured **regiment**. It is commanded by a Colonel and has its own name, history and traditions. It looks after its own discipline, pay, rations and training, and it can be moved from one formation to another without losing its identity.

A **sub-unit** is a part of a unit: a **company**, **platoon** or **section** (squadron, troop or battery in other arms). It has no administration of its own and depends on the unit.

A **formation** is a headquarters that commands a *group of units* of different kinds: **Brigade, Division, Corps and Command**. A formation has no fixed set of units for ever; units are added and removed as the task demands. A brigade today may have one tank squadron attached, and tomorrow none.

> **Remember.** Units are the *bricks*; formations are the *buildings* made from them. A brick can be moved from one building to another.

## Chain of Command and the Idea of Control

The **chain of command** is the line along which orders pass down and reports pass up. In the Army it runs from the President through the government to the COAS, then through the Army Commander, Corps Commander, Division Commander, Brigade Commander and Commanding Officer, to the Company, Platoon and Section Commanders. Each person gives orders to the one below and answers to the one above, and no level is skipped in normal working.

**Command** is the *legal authority and responsibility* to direct people and to be held accountable for them. A Commanding Officer has command of his battalion: he can reward and punish, and he is answerable for everything it does or fails to do. **Control** is the narrower power to *direct and coordinate the actions* of forces in order to carry out a plan. Control can be given without command.

The distinction matters daily. Suppose a tank squadron is sent to help an infantry brigade. The brigade commander has *operational control*: he tells the squadron where to go and what to attack. But the squadron's own commanding officer still looks after its pay, discipline, repairs and welfare. The squadron has been *lent*, not *given away*. This idea of lending forces up and down the chain is the key to understanding the next chapter.
