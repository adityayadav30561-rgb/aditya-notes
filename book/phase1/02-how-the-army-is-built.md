# How the Army Is Built
~ Levels, attachments and who joins whom, at which level

## The Ladder from Army HQ to the Section

The Indian Army is built like a ladder with nine rungs. At the top is **Army Headquarters**, which directs the whole Army. Below it the Army is split into **seven Commands**, each looking after a region. Each Command controls several **Corps**; each Corps controls two or three **Divisions**; each Division controls three or four **Brigades**; each Brigade controls about three **Battalions**. A battalion is made of **Companies**, a company of **Platoons**, and a platoon of **Sections**.

The next page shows the ladder with the commander, rank and strength of each rung. Study it first, then read on: the interesting part is not the ladder itself but how the *different arms* (tanks, guns, signals, engineers, air defence) fit into it.

## Own and Lend: the Big Idea

If every level contained only infantry, the Army would be easy to understand. In reality, a modern battle needs many arms working together, so each level of the Army holds two kinds of force.

1. **What it owns.** Units that belong permanently to that level's order of battle. A division owns its artillery brigade, its engineer regiment and its signal regiment. A corps owns its own artillery, air defence and engineers as well.
2. **What it lends downward.** Out of the units it owns, a commander *allots* some to the level below for a particular task: a field regiment to a brigade, a battery to a battalion, a tank squadron to an infantry attack. The loan ends when the task ends, and the unit goes back to its parent.

Because of this, the same battalion may fight one day with no support and the next day with a battery, a tank troop and an engineer section beside it. Nobody changes the battalion's own organisation. Only the *task organisation* changes.

### The words used for lending

- **Under command:** the receiving commander has full authority over the unit for the task.
- **Operational control (OPCON):** he can tell the unit *what* to do, but cannot re-organise it or break it up; its parent still looks after administration.
- **Attached:** the unit is placed with the receiving formation, usually for administration as well as for tasks.
- **In support:** the unit helps the formation but stays under its own commander, who decides how best to do it.
- **Direct support (DS):** an artillery unit has the job of answering the call of one particular formation first, for example "a field regiment in direct support of 5 Brigade".
- **General support (GS):** artillery fires for the whole formation, and its commander decides where its fire is most useful.

## Are Corps and Divisions Mostly Infantry?

For most of the Indian Army, yes. A typical **infantry or mountain division** has about nine infantry battalions (three brigades of three battalions each). That is the core, and it is why people say a division is "infantry-based". But the infantry is only part of the picture. The division also *owns*, permanently, an artillery brigade, an engineer regiment, a signal regiment, an air defence regiment, an armoured reconnaissance element, supply, repair and medical units. These are called **divisional troops**. They are not "attached" in the temporary sense; they belong to the division.

A **corps** is a larger version of the same idea. It is made of divisions, plus **corps troops**: an artillery brigade, an air defence brigade, an engineer brigade, a signal regiment, army aviation and independent brigades. Whether a corps is "infantry-heavy" or "armour-heavy" depends on the divisions placed in it. A **holding corps** is mostly infantry and mountain divisions. A **strike corps** is built around armoured and mechanised divisions.

> **Not everything is infantry-based.** There are divisions whose core is *not* infantry. An **Armoured Division** is built around armoured brigades. A **Mechanised (RAPID) Division** is built around mechanised infantry and armour. An **Artillery Division** is a formation of artillery brigades, held at a higher level to give concentrated fire to a corps (verify). So the answer to "is it infantry with other things attached?" is: *usually, but it depends on the division type.*

## Armoured Division: Only Tanks?

No. An armoured division does *not* consist of armoured vehicles alone, and it could not fight if it did. Tanks are powerful but they are blind in close country, they are vulnerable to infantry carrying anti-tank missiles, and they cannot hold ground, clear minefields or drive off aircraft by themselves.

So an armoured division is a complete **combined-arms team**. Its core is two or three **armoured brigades** (each with three armoured regiments and a mechanised infantry battalion, indicatively) and a **mechanised infantry** element that rides in infantry combat vehicles so that it can keep up with the tanks. On top of this the division owns its own **artillery brigade** (usually self-propelled guns that can move with the tanks), **air defence**, **engineers** (to breach minefields and build bridges), **signals**, **reconnaissance**, and **supply and repair units** (a tank needs fuel, shells and constant mechanical attention). An armoured division therefore holds a lot of support troops alongside its tanks, but it has fewer *infantry battalions* than an infantry division.

## Artillery in the Division and Corps

Artillery does not appear at these levels as a loose collection of guns. It is organised in layers.

- A **gun** is operated by a detachment.
- A **battery** is the sub-unit: about six guns under a Major.
- A **regiment** is the unit: three batteries, about eighteen guns, under a Colonel. There are field regiments, medium regiments, light and mountain regiments, and rocket regiments.
- An **artillery brigade** is the formation: several regiments under a Brigadier.

At the **division**, the artillery is a **Divisional Artillery Brigade** (verify the exact designation). At the **corps**, it is a larger **Corps Artillery Brigade** with heavier guns, rocket and missile regiments. So to the question "is it a brigade, a battery or what?", the answer is that the artillery *owned* at division and corps level is a **brigade**, made of regiments, which are made of batteries.

### How artillery then joins the lower levels

Artillery is almost never used as one lump. The commander of the artillery brigade breaks it up and allots pieces downward:

1. A **regiment** goes in **direct support** of an infantry brigade.
2. A **battery** goes with one infantry battalion; the **Battery Commander** (a Major) lives beside the CO as his artillery adviser.
3. A **Forward Observation Officer (FOO)** party goes with each assaulting company to see the enemy and call fire onto him.
4. What remains stays in **general support** of the whole division, and the brigade commander can call for it through the divisional artillery commander.

This is why artillery is the best example of the "own and lend" idea. The whole brigade belongs to the division, but pieces of it are lent down to brigades, battalions and companies, and can be pulled back and re-lent as the battle changes.

## Signals: How They Join

Signals work differently from other arms, and it is a good thing to understand. There is no big Signals battalion that marches with an infantry brigade. Instead, Signals units are built to match the *headquarters* they serve.

- The **Corps Signal Regiment** serves the Corps HQ and links it to the divisions.
- The **Divisional Signal Regiment** serves the Division HQ and links it to the brigades.
- A **Brigade Signal Company**, detached from the divisional signal regiment, lives with the Brigade HQ and links it to its battalions.
- At **battalion** level there is no Corps of Signals unit at all. The infantry battalion has its *own* signal platoon, manned by infantrymen trained in communications.

So Signals do not "belong" to an infantry brigade in the way a battalion does. They are *detached* from the signal regiment at the next level up, and they move with the HQ they support. When a brigade is moved to another division, its signal company goes with it.

## Air Defence and Armour: At What Level?

**Air defence** follows the same pattern as artillery. A **corps** owns an air defence brigade or several regiments; a **division** owns an air defence regiment; and a regiment's **batteries** are allotted downward to protect brigades, bridges, headquarters and gun positions. An infantry battalion on the move may have a small **detachment** with shoulder-fired missiles. Air defence units are therefore parts of a bigger unit that are repeatedly lent to the units below.

**Armour** appears at three levels. A corps may own an **independent armoured brigade**. An armoured or RAPID division owns **armoured regiments** outright. And an infantry brigade in a hard attack may receive a **tank squadron** (about fourteen tanks) from an armoured regiment, lent for the battle, with perhaps a **tank troop** going with an assaulting battalion. When tanks and infantry go into battle together they form a **combat team** (or battle group) under one commander.

## Independent Brigades

A normal brigade belongs to a division. An **independent brigade** does not: it reports directly to a **corps** (or sometimes to a Command), and it carries its own supporting arms, so it is like a small division. There are independent infantry brigades, armoured brigades, artillery brigades, air defence brigades, engineer brigades and parachute brigades (for example, the 50th Independent Parachute Brigade at Agra).

A corps commander can use an independent brigade in three ways: hold it as a **reserve**, give it a **sector** of the front to defend on its own, or **lend it to a division** as an extra brigade. When the division's task is over, the brigade goes back to the corps.

## The Newer Formations: Rudra, Bhairav and IBGs

The Army is currently experimenting with new, more flexible organisations (verify details). **Rudra** brigades are all-arms brigades with infantry, armour, artillery, engineers and drones in one formation, so that they can fight on their own. **Bhairav** battalions are light commando units for fast, specialised operations. **Integrated Battle Groups (IBGs)** are brigade-sized, all-arms groups that combine the arms permanently, rather than gathering them by lending when war starts. The idea behind all three is the same: to put the lending of arms into the *peacetime* organisation so that the groups can move faster when war begins.
