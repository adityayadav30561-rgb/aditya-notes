# The Army Commands
~ Seven regional headquarters, and what each one looks after

[[wide]]
## The Seven Commands

India's land borders are very long and very different, so the Army is divided into **seven Commands**, six of which are operational and look after a region, and one of which trains. Each Command is headed by an **Army Commander**.

### The Seven Commands at a Glance

| Command | Headquarters | Area it looks after | Remark |
| Northern | Udhampur | Jammu and Kashmir, Ladakh | Faces both Pakistan (LoC) and China (LAC); includes Siachen |
| Western | Chandimandir | Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, parts of Haryana | Plains border with Pakistan; large strike potential |
| South Western | Jaipur | Rajasthan, parts of Gujarat | Desert warfare; raised in 2005 |
| Southern | Pune | Gujarat, Maharashtra and southern states | Largest by area; oldest command (1895) |
| Central | Lucknow | Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Bihar and others | Heartland command; also looks after central sector of the LAC |
| Eastern | Kolkata | Sikkim, North-East, West Bengal | Faces China (LAC) and Myanmar and Bangladesh borders |
| Army Training Command (ARTRAC) | Shimla | No operational area | Doctrine and training for the whole Army |
[[/wide]]

## The Army Commander

An Army Command is headed by the **General Officer Commanding-in-Chief (GOC-in-C)**, usually called the **Army Commander**. His rank is **Lieutenant General**. He is one of the most senior officers in the Army, second in importance only to the COAS, and he answers to the COAS for everything that happens in his Command: its readiness, its training, its welfare and, in war, its operations.

An Army Commander does not command troops directly. He commands through his **Corps Commanders**, who in turn command their divisions. This makes the Command the highest field headquarters.

## The Operational Commands

Six Commands are *operational*: they would fight the war in their region. Their areas follow the borders and the threats along them.

- **Northern Command (Udhampur):** the busiest. It faces the LoC with Pakistan in J&K, the LAC with China in Ladakh, and holds the Siachen Glacier. It has several corps, including those in Srinagar, Nagrota and Leh (verify).
- **Western Command (Chandimandir):** the plains border with Pakistan in Punjab. A large part of the Army's offensive power in the plains is based here.
- **South Western Command (Jaipur):** the Rajasthan desert. Armoured and mechanised forces fight here over open ground.
- **Southern Command (Pune):** the largest Command by area. It covers the desert and coastal border of Gujarat, the south of the country and coastal defence in cooperation with the Navy.
- **Central Command (Lucknow):** the central heartland. It trains and holds forces in the middle of the country, watches the **central sector of the LAC** (Uttarakhand and Himachal) and gives help to the civil authority inside the country. It has fewer fighting formations than the border Commands, and some of its forces act as a reserve that can be sent where needed (verify).
- **Eastern Command (Kolkata):** the north-eastern frontier. It covers Sikkim, Arunachal Pradesh and the North-East, facing China, and it also handles counter-insurgency in the North-East.

> **Why does the Army have regional Commands?** The borders are thousands of kilometres long and the terrain changes from glaciers to desert to jungle. A single headquarters could not understand and manage all of it. A regional Command gives local knowledge, faster decisions and one responsible commander for each region.

## Army Training Command

The **Army Training Command (ARTRAC)** in **Shimla** is not an operational Command. It looks after the training of the *whole Army*, not of a region. Its job is to write the Army's **doctrine** (how the Army thinks it should fight), study the lessons of wars and exercises, and set the standards for how the Army is trained.

Whom does it train? It runs the Army's big training schools: the **Infantry School** at Mhow, the **Armoured Corps Centre and School** at Ahmednagar, the **School of Artillery** at Devlali, the **College of Military Engineering** at Pune and similar schools for the other arms. These schools train officers, JCOs and soldiers of every arm. ARTRAC also looks after the Army's training academies and keeps the syllabus up to date (verify the exact schools under it, as arrangements change).

## Which Command for Which Border

An interviewer often asks "who looks after this border?". The simplest answer is to follow the map from west to east.

- **Pakistan border, plains:** Western and South Western Commands. **LoC in J&K:** Northern Command.
- **China border (LAC), Ladakh:** Northern Command. **Central sector (Uttarakhand, Himachal):** Central Command. **Sikkim, Arunachal Pradesh:** Eastern Command.
- **North-East (Nagaland, Manipur, Assam and so on):** Eastern Command, including counter-insurgency.
- **Coast and southern India:** Southern Command, working with the Navy.

Command boundaries are not fixed for ever. They are reorganised when threats change; the **South Western Command**, for example, was created in 2005 by taking an area from the Western Command, to give a closer watch on the Rajasthan border.

## Army Command and Corps: the Difference

Think of an Army Command as a *state government* and a Corps as a *district administration*. The state government looks after a whole region, makes the broad plan and gives resources to the districts, but it does not run the day-to-day work in the field. The districts, with their own officials and staff, do the actual work on the ground.

In the same way:
- A **Command** is the *highest field headquarters*. It covers a whole region and controls **several corps**, plus other formations such as independent brigades, a sub-area and its stations. It plans, allots resources and takes the large decisions. It does not fight battles in person.
- A **Corps** is a *fighting formation*. It has its own troops: **two to three divisions** and the corps troops (artillery, engineers, signals and so on). It fights the battle in its sector. Its commander is a Lieutenant General, just like the Army Commander, but he is lower in the chain.

**Example.** Northern Command (Udhampur) looks after J&K and Ladakh. Under it, the Chinar Corps (XV Corps, Srinagar) holds the Kashmir valley; the White Knight Corps (XVI Corps, Nagrota) holds the Jammu area; and the Fire and Fury Corps (XIV Corps, Leh) holds Ladakh (verify). Northern Command decides how many troops and resources each corps gets. Each corps commander then decides how to fight within his area.

> **To say in an interview.** "A Command is a regional headquarters under a Lt Gen that controls several corps. A Corps is a fighting formation of two to three divisions, also under a Lt Gen, which fights the battle in its sector."
