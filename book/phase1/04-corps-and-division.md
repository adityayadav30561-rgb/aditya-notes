# Corps and Division
~ The two large fighting formations: what they are, how they differ, and how they are made up

## The Corps

A **Corps** is a large fighting formation that sits between a Command and a Division. It is the highest level at which the Army actually *fights* battles. It is made up of **two to three divisions** plus **corps troops** (units that belong to the corps itself), and there are about **14 corps** in the Army (verify the exact number, as new ones are raised from time to time).

A corps is commanded by a **General Officer Commanding (GOC)** of the rank of **Lieutenant General**, who is the Corps Commander. His headquarters, the **Corps HQ**, makes the plan for the corps' sector, controls the divisions and gives them their orders, and allots the corps' supporting arms to wherever they are most needed.

A corps is not the same everywhere. Its shape depends on its **role and terrain**. A corps in the Kashmir valley or in Ladakh is a mountain corps, with mountain divisions; a corps in the plains of Punjab or Rajasthan may have armoured and mechanised forces; a corps in the North-East is trained and equipped for jungle and high mountains.

### Corps troops

Besides its divisions, the corps owns a number of units directly: **artillery** (an artillery brigade with heavier guns and rockets than the divisions have), **air defence**, **engineers** (an engineer brigade with bridging and mine-clearing equipment), **signals** (the Corps Signal Regiment), **army aviation**, **intelligence** and **logistic units** such as supply, repair and medical. These are the **corps troops**.

Why does the corps keep them? Because some things are needed only occasionally, or in large numbers at one point. Heavy artillery and bridging equipment would be wasted if scattered equally among all divisions. The corps keeps them together and *lends* them to whichever division needs them at the moment. It is the same "own and lend" idea explained in the previous chapter.

## Corps and Division: the Difference

A **Corps** is bigger and higher: it has **several divisions** and is commanded by a **Lieutenant General**. A **Division** is smaller: it has **three or four brigades** and is commanded by a **Major General**.

A simple comparison: a corps is like a *district* with several *sub-divisions*; a division is like one sub-division, with its own officers and staff that run it. The corps commander decides *what* each division must achieve; the divisional commander decides *how*.

## The Strike Corps and the Holding Corps

Corps have different jobs. The two most important types are strike corps and holding corps.

### The Holding Corps

A **Holding Corps** is a *defensive* corps. It is stationed along the border all the time, in its fixed defences. In war, its task is to *hold* the ground against the enemy's first attack and to absorb it, so that the enemy cannot break through and the rest of the Army has time to mobilise. It is mostly **infantry-heavy**; it has infantry and mountain divisions, with artillery, engineers and signals in support. Examples are the **XV Corps (Srinagar)**, the **XVI Corps (Nagrota)**, the **XIV Corps (Leh)**, and the **XI Corps (Jalandhar)** (verify).

### The Strike Corps

A **Strike Corps** is an *offensive* corps. It is kept back from the border, in reserve, until the Army decides the moment has come to attack. Then it moves forward and strikes deep into enemy territory, aiming to destroy the enemy's forces and to capture important ground. It is built around **armoured and mechanised divisions** that move fast, with strong artillery, air defence and engineer support. Examples are the **I Corps (Mathura)**, the **II Corps (Ambala)** and the **XXI Corps (Bhopal)** (verify). The **XVII Corps (Panagarh)** is a **mountain strike corps** raised for the northern borders (verify).

> **Remember.** Holding corps *hold* and are infantry-heavy and stay at the border. Strike corps *strike* and are armour-heavy and are kept in reserve. A holding corps is like a wall; a strike corps is like a fist.

### The Corps Reserve

Inside each corps, the Corps Commander keeps back some forces, often an independent brigade or a battalion with tanks, that he has not committed to the battle. This is the **corps reserve**. He uses it to meet a crisis (for example, if the enemy breaks through at one point) or to exploit success (if his troops win an advantage, to push it further).

## The Division

A **Division** is the major tactical formation of the Army, with three or four brigades and supporting arms, and about **15,000 to 18,000** troops. A division is commanded by a **General Officer Commanding (GOC)**, a **Major General**. The **Division HQ** controls its brigades, decides their tasks and arranges fire, supplies and communications.

In a battle a division is given an *area* to hold or a *position* to capture. The GOC studies the ground and the enemy, gives each brigade its task, and then holds a reserve to deal with surprises.

### Divisional troops

A division, like a corps, owns units directly: a **divisional artillery brigade**, an **engineer regiment**, a **signal regiment**, an **air defence regiment**, an **armoured reconnaissance** element, an **army aviation** flight and its own **supply, repair and medical** units. These are the **divisional troops**, and the GOC allots them to his brigades as the battle needs.

## Types of Division and Their Strength

No, not every division is the same. The Army has several kinds, built for different jobs, and their strength and shape differ.

### Divisions Compared

| Type | Core of the division | Typical job | Notes |
| Infantry division | About 3 infantry brigades (9 infantry battalions) | Hold or attack in plains and semi-hilly ground | The most common type; around 15,000 troops |
| Mountain division | 3 or 4 mountain infantry brigades | High-altitude operations: Ladakh, Kashmir, Sikkim, Arunachal | Lighter equipment; mules, porters, light vehicles, helicopters; about 12,000 to 15,000 troops (verify) |
| Armoured division | 2 or 3 armoured brigades plus a mechanised infantry element | Deep, fast offensive actions | Several hundred tanks; owns self-propelled artillery and engineers; fewer infantry battalions |
| Mechanised (RAPID) division | Mechanised infantry brigades plus armour | Fast, protected movement, with infantry in vehicles | Infantry combat vehicles keep up with the tanks |
| Artillery division | Artillery brigades | Concentrated fire for a corps | Held at a higher level, not part of a corps' divisions (verify) |

### Infantry Division and Mountain Division

An **Infantry Division** is built around infantry brigades and is suited to the plains and gentle hills. A **Mountain Division** is also infantry-based, but it is designed and trained for **high altitude**. It travels light: it uses mule and porter transport, helicopters, light vehicles and lighter weapons, because a heavy tank or truck cannot climb the tracks. Its soldiers are acclimatised to cold and thin air and trained in mountain warfare. Examples are the 3rd Infantry Division (Leh) and the 8th Mountain Division in the Dras and Kargil area (verify).

The difference in one sentence: *infantry divisions fight best on the plains; mountain divisions are lighter, move on foot, and are trained for high altitude.*

### Armoured Division, Mechanised Division and Artillery Division

An **Armoured Division** is built around armoured brigades of tanks with mechanised infantry, for fast offensive action. The 1st Armoured Division at Patiala and the 31st Armoured Division at Jhansi are examples (verify). A **Mechanised (RAPID) Division** is the next step: infantry who ride in armoured vehicles so that they can move with the tanks, while keeping the full strength of an infantry formation. An **Artillery Division** is a formation of artillery brigades that can bring huge concentrated fire to bear on a point (verify).

## Role of a Division in Operations

In war, a division fights the battle in its area: it controls its brigades, gets fire support from its artillery, uses its engineers to build or breach obstacles, and keeps up communications and supply. A division's most important quality is the ability to *change its shape quickly*, by taking in extra battalions, tank squadrons, or artillery, as the battle develops.
