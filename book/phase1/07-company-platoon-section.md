# Company, Platoon and Section
~ The small groups where officers first lead soldiers

## The Company

A **Company** is a sub-unit of a battalion with three platoons and a headquarters, about **100 to 120** men. It sits below the Battalion and above the Platoon. It is commanded by a **Company Commander**, normally a **Major**, assisted by a **Second-in-Command** (a Captain) and a small headquarters that includes the **Company Havildar Major (CHM)**, the senior NCO who runs the company's discipline and routine, and the **Company Quartermaster Havildar (CQMH)**, who looks after its stores. Signallers and runners complete the group.

The Company Commander leads, trains and looks after his company and fights it in battle. His Second-in-Command helps him and takes over if he is hurt or absent. A Company Commander commands about 120 men; a Commanding Officer commands about 800.

### Kinds of company

- A **Rifle Company** has three rifle platoons and is the basic fighting company of the battalion.
- A **Support Company** (the battalion's HQ company) holds the heavier weapons: mortars, anti-tank missiles and machine guns. It supports the rifle companies with fire.

A company can operate **independently** for a limited task, for example holding a forward post or a patrol base, provided it has support. When a company is sent away from the battalion for a task of its own, it is called a **detached** or **independent company**. At company level the supporting elements may include a mortar section, machine guns, rocket launchers or anti-tank missiles, snipers and radios, and, for an attack, a **Forward Observation Officer** who calls artillery fire.

## The Platoon

A **Platoon** is a sub-unit of a company with three sections and a headquarters, about **30 to 35** men. It sits below the Company and above the Section. It is commanded by a **Platoon Commander**: normally a **Lieutenant** or a **Captain**, or sometimes a **JCO** (a Subedar or Naib Subedar). The headquarters of the platoon has the Platoon Commander, a **Platoon Havildar** (the senior NCO), a radio operator and a runner.

The platoon is the first command of a newly commissioned officer, and it is where he learns to lead on the ground. The officer is responsible for the training, fitness, welfare and discipline of his men. He is helped by the Platoon Havildar, an experienced NCO who knows each man and runs the platoon's routine.

### What the platoon commander does

In training he looks after **fitness, weapon handling, firing, field craft and discipline**. In the field he **plans, leads, keeps his men safe, completes the mission and reports** to his Company Commander. A platoon attacks, defends, patrols, lays ambushes or holds a post, as part of the company's plan. Small independent tasks, such as a patrol or an ambush, are often given to a platoon.

The difference with the Company Commander is mainly scale: the Platoon Commander (a Lieutenant) leads about 30 men whom he knows by name; the Company Commander (a Major) leads about 120, through his platoon commanders.

## The Section

A **Section** is the smallest sub-unit of the infantry, about **10 soldiers**, and sits below the Platoon. Three sections make a platoon. It is commanded by a **Section Commander**, who is a **Havildar** or a **Naik**. He leads the section, controls its fire and movement, and looks after his men.

### Inside a section

A section is divided into two small groups, or **fire teams** of four or five men each:
- the **rifle group**, with rifles and an under-barrel grenade launcher; and
- the **fire support group** (the light machine gun group), with the **Light Machine Gun (LMG)**, which gives the section sustained covering fire while the rifle group moves.

The section's weapons are the standard rifle (INSAS, AK-203 or SIG-716), the LMG, grenades and the under-barrel grenade launcher. The section is the smallest *tactical* group in the infantry. The **rifleman** is the basic infantry soldier; he fights with his rifle and carries out the section's tasks. The **LMG operator** gives covering fire, so that the rest of the section can move.

> **Why the platoon matters to a young officer.** It is the first place where he leads men directly. He learns to command real soldiers, earns their trust and sees how the Army works at the lowest level, before he moves up.
