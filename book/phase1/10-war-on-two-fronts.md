# War on Two Fronts
~ One imagined war, followed from Army HQ down to the Section

## How to Read This Chapter

This chapter ties everything together. It follows an *imaginary* war in which India has to fight on two borders at once: against Pakistan along the **Line of Control (LoC)** and the plains, and against China along the **Line of Actual Control (LAC)** in the mountains. The corps and divisions named here are real, but the story is only an example, and the names and locations are indicative (verify). The aim is to show how the levels you have learned about work together, and how arms such as tanks, signals and air defence plug into them.

## Day Zero: the Situation

After weeks of rising tension, firing starts along the LoC in J&K and, at the same time, Chinese troops move forward in Ladakh and on the eastern border. The Government orders the Armed Forces to war footing. India now has two fronts, and the Army must hold both while keeping the means to hit back hard on one of them.

## Army Headquarters Gives the Order

At Army HQ in New Delhi, the COAS, advised by the DGMO and the staff branches, receives the Government's direction. He issues a directive to each Command: what the aim is, what forces are available and when to be ready. The Army Commanders in turn give orders to their Corps Commanders. Three branches of Army HQ work at once: **operations** plans the fight, the **Quartermaster General** moves fuel and rations to the front, and the **Master General of Ordnance** speeds up ammunition, spares and weapons.

At this stage, **no unit is moved without a reason**. The strike corps in the plains (I Corps, Mathura; II Corps, Ambala; XXI Corps, Bhopal) and the mountain strike corps (XVII Corps, Panagarh) stay in reserve. They will move only when the COAS and the Government have decided where to strike. The holding corps are already on the border. (All verify.)

## On the LoC: the Holding Corps at Work

Suppose the enemy attacks across the LoC in the Kashmir valley. The part of the border they hit is held by **XV Corps (Srinagar)** of Northern Command, and within it by a division such as the **19th Infantry Division (Baramulla)** (verify). Let us follow the chain from the bottom.

**A platoon** on a forward post sees movement at night. The Platoon Commander, a young Lieutenant, reports by radio to his **Company Commander**. The Company Commander tells the **Commanding Officer** of the battalion, who tells the **Brigade Commander**. In a few minutes, this report has reached the brigade headquarters, and the GOC of the division soon knows.

**Fire flows down.** The **Forward Observation Officer** with the company calls the guns. The **battery** attached to the battalion answers first; if more is needed, the **field regiment in direct support** of the brigade joins in; if the attack is heavy, the division's artillery commander brings in the **medium regiments** from the divisional artillery brigade, and the corps may lend its own heavy guns and rockets. All this fire is coordinated through the **signal links** that the Signals provide: the **brigade signal company** joins brigade HQ to its battalions; the **divisional signal regiment** joins the division to the brigades.

**Air defence.** The **air defence regiment** of the division sends a **battery** to cover the brigade headquarters and gun positions, and small detachments with shoulder-fired missiles go forward with the battalions.

### Calling for more brigades

The enemy breaks into one sector and the brigade's three battalions are all committed. The Brigade Commander tells the GOC he needs more troops. The GOC has a **reserve brigade** or battalion, which he moves forward. If that is not enough, he asks the Corps Commander, who sends an **independent brigade** from the corps reserve. The independent brigade comes **under command** of the division for the battle, together with its own artillery and engineers, and its **signal detachment** links up with the division's signal net on arrival. The fight is held. When it ends, the independent brigade goes back to the corps.

## On the LAC in Ladakh: the Mountain Divisions

The Chinese attack in Ladakh, where **XIV Corps (Leh)** holds the front with divisions such as the **3rd Infantry Division** (verify). The ground is a high-altitude desert. The troops are thin on the ground, because few men can live at 15,000 feet, and movement is slow. So the Army depends on the things it has brought with it:

- **Mountain brigades** hold the passes and heights. They move on foot, with mules and light vehicles, carrying light weapons.
- **Helicopters** of Army Aviation bring ammunition and carry casualties.
- **Engineers** keep roads and bridges open under shellfire.
- **Armoured regiments** with tanks that work in thin air can be sent to the flatter ground near the lakes, as they were in Ladakh in 2020 (verify). A tank squadron comes under the operational control of the brigade it supports.
- **Air defence** units guard Leh airfield and the supply routes; the Corps Air Defence Brigade lends batteries to the divisions.
- **Signals**: the Corps Signal Regiment links the corps HQ; the Divisional Signal Regiment links the division to its brigades; each brigade has its signal company, which uses radio relays on mountain peaks to cover the ground.

### The mountain division in the East

In the east, **XXXIII Corps (Siliguri)** holds Sikkim and North Bengal with mountain divisions such as the **17th Mountain Division (Gangtok)** and **27th Mountain Division (Kalimpong)**, and further east **IV Corps (Tezpur)** and **III Corps (Dimapur)** hold Arunachal Pradesh (verify). A mountain division here has the same pattern: three infantry brigades, a divisional artillery with light and mountain guns, engineers and signals. Because supply roads are few, the division pre-stocks ammunition and food in forward depots, and **the engineers become as important as the infantry**: a bridge washed away means a brigade cut off.

## On the Plains: the Strike Corps

Meanwhile in the plains of Punjab and Rajasthan, the **holding corps** (such as XI Corps, Jalandhar) hold the border while the enemy tries to cross. After the first attacks have been held, the COAS and the Government decide to hit back. A **strike corps** is released, for example **II Corps** at Ambala (verify).

A strike corps does not simply drive forward as a crowd of tanks. It goes as a column of many arms.

1. **Armoured regiments** lead, with tanks, supported by **mechanised infantry** in armoured vehicles, who protect the tanks from enemy infantry and clear the ground.
2. **Self-propelled artillery** moves with them and fires from the move.
3. **Air defence** units with guns and missiles protect the column from aircraft.
4. **Engineers** carry **bridging equipment** and **mine-clearing** vehicles to cross canals and breach minefields.
5. **Signals** keep radio contact between the corps, divisions and brigades, using radio and satellite links.
6. **Supply, repair and medical units** follow behind with fuel, shells and spare parts.

The corps commander and his divisions plan the advance, and he holds back an **independent armoured brigade** as his reserve. When the attack has reached its objective, the strike corps hands over the captured ground to a holding formation, pulls back, repairs its tanks, restocks its ammunition and gets ready for the next move.

## Where Everything Plugs In

The table at the end of this chapter gives a one-page summary. In words, the main rule is: **an arm is owned at one level and lent down to the levels below.**

- **Infantry battalions** are the basic bricks. A brigade is made of three; a division of three to four brigades. They do not need to be lent.
- **Tanks** are owned by armoured regiments, which are grouped into armoured brigades. A tank squadron is lent down to an infantry brigade, a troop to a battalion.
- **Mechanised infantry** is owned by mechanised battalions in armoured and RAPID formations; a mechanised company is often "married" with a tank squadron to make a combat team.
- **Artillery** is owned as a brigade at the division and at the corps; regiments go in direct support to brigades, batteries to battalions, and FOOs to companies.
- **Air defence** is owned as a regiment at the division and as a brigade at the corps; batteries are lent down to protect brigades and key points.
- **Signals** are owned as a regiment at the corps and at the division; the brigade signal company is detached to the brigade HQ. A battalion has its own signal platoon of infantrymen.
- **Engineers** are owned as a brigade at the corps and a regiment at the division; an engineer field company is affiliated to a brigade, and sections are lent to battalions for special tasks.

## The War Level by Level

| Level | What it does in this war | Who it works with |
| Army HQ | Sets the aim, gives the directive, decides where the strike corps will be used | Government, Navy, Air Force, all Commands |
| Command | Divides forces among its corps, fights the regional battle | Corps and independent brigades |
| Holding Corps | Holds the border, absorbs the first blow, gives guns and brigades to its divisions | Divisions, corps artillery, engineers, air defence |
| Strike Corps | Launches the counter-offensive when released | Armoured and mechanised divisions, independent armoured brigade |
| Division | Fights the battle in its sector; allots its artillery and engineers to its brigades | Brigades, divisional troops |
| Brigade | Defends a sector or captures an objective; coordinates guns, engineers and tanks | Three battalions and attached arms |
| Battalion | Holds a locality or attacks an objective; fires mortars and anti-tank missiles from its support company | Four rifle companies, attached battery and tanks |
| Company | Holds a post or attacks a position with its platoons | Platoons, FOO, mortar and machine gun detachments |
| Platoon | Patrols, ambushes, defends a post or leads an assault | Three sections |
| Section | Fights with rifle and LMG; uses fire and movement | Rifle group and fire support group |

## After the Battle

When the fighting slows, the units are reorganised. Lent units return to their parents: the tank squadron to its regiment, the battery to its field regiment, the signal detachment to its signal regiment, the independent brigade to the corps. Casualties are replaced, ammunition is restocked, and the tired holding units are relieved by fresh ones. The chain of command, which looked so complicated in the middle of the fight, is restored to its peacetime shape.

> **The simple lesson.** Every level owns something and lends something. Orders go down the chain; reports go up. Fire, supplies and reinforcements are lent downward where the battle needs them most, and returned when it is over. A platoon commander and an Army Commander are doing the same job on different scales.
