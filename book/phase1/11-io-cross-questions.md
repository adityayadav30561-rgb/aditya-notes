# Questions the IO May Ask
~ Cross-questions on command, leadership and your first posting as an officer

## What You Will Command as a Lieutenant

If you are commissioned as a Lieutenant, your first command will be a **platoon** of about **30 to 35 soldiers**, made of three sections, with a Platoon Havildar and a JCO to help you. You will be answerable to your Company Commander, a Major. Your immediate juniors are the Platoon Havildar and the three Section Commanders (Havildars or Naiks); in an armoured regiment you would command a troop of tanks.

### A typical day

Your day starts with **physical training** with the platoon, followed by **training**: weapon handling, firing, field craft and drill. You check the men's turnout, weapons and barracks; you attend to their problems; and you carry out any duties given by the Company Commander. In the evening you take part in games and sports. Training, welfare and discipline fill the day.

## Men, JCOs and NCOs: What to Expect

Your men will expect **fairness, care, competence and courage**, and that you will lead by example. They follow an officer willingly into danger only if they *trust* him.

From your **JCOs** you should expect experience and guidance, discipline and a link with the men. From your **NCOs** you should expect supervision of the men on the ground and the setting of standards. A young Lieutenant needs them because they have years of experience: they know the men, the terrain and the unit's customs. The wise officer listens to them and then takes the decision himself.

## Discipline, Morale and Training

### Discipline

You maintain discipline through **fair and consistent rules**, personal example, and a balance of reward and punishment. Men who see their officer keeping to the same standards respect his orders.

### Morale

You maintain morale through **welfare, sports, recognition** and honest communication, and by leading from the front. Soldiers have high morale when they know they are cared for.

### Training

You train your platoon in **fitness, weapon handling, field craft, drill and tactical exercises**, beginning with the basics and increasing the difficulty gradually. After each exercise you review what went right and wrong.

## Handling Difficulties

- **A soldier who keeps failing the standard.** Find out why. Give extra training and support. If there is still no improvement after a fair chance, take formal action.
- **A soldier whose personal problem affects his work.** Talk to him privately, help to solve it (leave, help for his family, welfare), and so keep him productive. A soldier who knows his officer cares will give more.
- **Disagreement with your Platoon Havildar.** Listen: he has more experience. Discuss it in private, not in front of the men. Then take the final decision and own it.
- **Your platoon fails in an exercise.** Analyse what went wrong, take the responsibility yourself, correct the mistakes, retrain and try again.
- **Your platoon does exceptionally well.** Appreciate and reward them in front of others, share the credit, and keep the standard high.

## Leadership Ideas

### Authority and leadership

**Authority** is the power that comes with rank; people obey because they must. **Leadership** is the ability to make people follow you willingly. A good officer has both, but depends on leadership.

### "Lead from the front"

It means the officer leads in person: he shares the hardship, and he goes first into danger. His men follow him because he is with them.

### What makes a good Platoon Commander

Competence, fairness, care for his men, courage, integrity and leading by example.

### An officer's first duty to his men

Their **welfare and safety** while the mission is accomplished. The Chetwode motto, displayed at the Indian Military Academy, says it well: the safety, honour and welfare of your country come first, always and every time; the honour, welfare and comfort of the men you command come next; your own ease, comfort and safety come last, always and every time.

## Why Army, Why SSC, Why OTA

### Why an officer, and why the Army

I want to **lead soldiers**, take responsibility and make decisions on the ground; that is the role of an officer. The Army offers a field career where I can lead men directly, and my fitness and leadership suit it.

### Why a Short Service Commission through the OTA

At my age, as a graduate, the **Short Service Commission** fits me. The training at the Officers Training Academy is a course of about 49 weeks. The commission is for ten years, with the option of a **Permanent Commission** later (verify the current terms).
